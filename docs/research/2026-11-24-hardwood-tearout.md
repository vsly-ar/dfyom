# Research: top-surface tearout in hardwood (first real cut, Setup 2)

Status: draft (2026-11-24; research for the user's request of 2026-11-24, Telegram: "first cut finished, please research how to reduce wood/hardwood tearing. Specifically the top surface got some tearing during pocketing or profiling (Setup 2)") · Base: [plan-v0.1.md § Pass strategies](../modes/plan-v0.1.md#pass-strategies-v01), [plan-v0.1.md § Toolpath generators](../modes/plan-v0.1.md#toolpath-generators-task-210), [feeds-speeds.md](../architecture/feeds-speeds.md), [library.md § Tool](../architecture/library.md#tool) · Terms: [glossary.md](../glossary.md)

The note reads what was actually cut (§ 1), collects what tool makers and woodworking references say about top-edge tearout (§ 2), gives settings for the next cut with the user's current tools (§ 3) and maps the findings onto DFYOM, ranked by value and effort (§ 4). ## 1. What was cut

Inputs, all read-only: the project `temp/enclosure-hardwood-250x100x40-two-setups.dfyom` (app 0.3.100, revision 93), its Setup 2 export `temp/Setup 2 – top (cavity, rim, outline).nc` (the newest export) and the user's tool and machine library. The plan operations have no entered values, no presets and no strategy changes, so every feed, speed, stepdown and stepover below is the **calculator's** ([auto mode](../modes/plan-v0.1.md#locks-and-auto-mode)), and every strategy value is a default.

- **Machine:** "3020 Nova", GRBL, fixed gantry, X/Y max feed **3000 mm/min**, Z 1000 mm/min, spindle 6200–29 300 rpm, 800 W.
- **Stock:** hardwood 250 × 100 × 40, Setup 2 top up, Z0 on the raw stock top. **The top was not faced**: Setup 2 has no facing operation, and the part's top is the stock's top, so no allowance is left to skim afterwards.
- **Tool type:** the library has no field for up-cut, down-cut or compression flutes. All four tools are entered as `flatEndMill` (end mills, not router bits). A generic 3-flute "finishing" end mill and generic 1- and 2-flute end mills are almost certainly **right-hand spiral, right-hand cut**, which lifts chips upwards (**up-cut**). This is not verified for these bits: the user can check whether the flutes twist like a normal screw thread when the bit is held point down.

How the numbers came about (all as documented): the hardwood row gives 0.127–0.178 mm/tooth for a 1/4″ tool ([feeds-speeds.md § Material data](../architecture/feeds-speeds.md#material-data), Onsrud/Amana at 18 000 rpm). At 18 000 rpm that chip load needs 6900–9600 mm/min, so the feed is capped at the machine's 3000 mm/min and the **spindle speed is lowered to keep the chip load** (DF56 rule, [feeds-speeds.md § Feed clamp](../architecture/feeds-speeds.md#feed-clamp-and-chip-load-hardening-df56)): 6350 rpm for 3 flutes and 9525 rpm for 2 flutes. With available power modelled as proportional to rpm, only about 140 W is left at 6350 rpm, so the power fit cuts the stepdown to 0.636 mm. The chip loads are inside the cited hardwood range: **the program does not rub, and it does not overfeed.**

Observations that bear on the top edge:

1. **Up-cut flutes on a visible top edge.** This is the textbook cause (§ 2.1). Every top edge in Setup 2 (cavity rim, outline, rebate, notch) is cut with up-cut flutes.
2. **Outline tool at 62 mm stickout** for a 31 mm cut. Deflection grows with the cube of unsupported length (§ 2.5), so that tool is about (62 / 36)³ ≈ **5×** more flexible than it needs to be. It is most flexible exactly at the **top levels**, where only the tip cuts, 62 mm from the collet. The earlier export (`…_setup2-top.nc`, 19:15) also cut the **cavity** with this 62 mm tool at 10 737 rpm and a 1.05 mm stepdown. If that file was the one run, deflection is the first suspect for the cavity rim too.
3. **The finishing allowance (0.2 mm) is thinner than typical tearout.** The rim is first cut by the roughing ring at R + 0.2 mm, in the first 0.64 mm level. Fibres torn there go deeper than 0.2 mm into the wall, so the finishing pass cannot remove them. References suggest 0.5–1 mm (§ 2.4).
4. **The finishing pass is one full-depth pass at the roughing feed.** At 0.2 mm radial on a 6.3 mm tool, radial chip thinning cuts the actual chip to about 0.157 × 2√(0.032 − 0.032²) ≈ **0.055 mm**, under half the hardwood minimum (0.127 mm). The pass leans toward rubbing on the exact surface that is seen. DFYOM's chip-thinning factor (RCTF) is applied to the roughing stepover only, and the `feeds.rubbing` check does not look at finishing passes. On this machine the feed cannot be raised (3000 mm/min cap), and the rpm cannot drop much (6350 against a 6200 minimum).
5. **Cavity deeper than the finishing tool's flutes:** 30.54 mm deep, 20 mm flutes in the library. The full-depth finishing pass then runs the shank against the wall over the last ~10 mm: the shank is Ø6.35 against a Ø6.33 cutter, so it rubs and burns the wall. This is not tearout, and it may be a library entry error (flute length). The pocket checks test depth ≤ stickout but not depth ≤ flute length ([plan-v0.1.md § Checks](../modes/plan-v0.1.md#tool-assignment-and-checks)). Also, 31.7 mm stickout for a 30.54 mm floor leaves about 1 mm between the collet nut and the stock top.
6. **Already good:** shallow levels (0.64 / 0.94 mm, well under the usual ½ D), helix and ramp entries rather than plunges, climb milling on every finishing pass (pocket walls counter-clockwise, outline clockwise, M3), and lead-in arcs on the finishing passes.

Not determined: grain direction (the stock has no grain field; likely along X, the 250 mm length), the species, the sharpness of the bits, and which walls tore. A photo would tell end-grain walls (the X ends) from long-grain walls.

## 2. What the sources say

### 2.1 Up-cut, down-cut, compression

- Up-cut spirals pull chips out but **lift and fray the top fibres**. Down-cut spirals hold the top fibres down and leave a clean top edge, but pack chips into the cut and fray the bottom edge. Compression bits are up-cut at the tip and down-cut above, so both faces come out clean ([Laguna: preventing tearout](https://info.lagunatools.com/how-to-prevent-tearout-on-a-wood-cnc-router), [Laguna: hardwood guide](https://info.lagunatools.com/cnc-milling-wood-a-guide-to-hardwood-tooling-feeds-and-clean-cuts), [WOODWEB: upcut, downcut or compression](https://woodweb.com/knowledge_base/Upcut_Downcut_Or_Compression_Tools.html), [Woodsmith: spiral flute bits](https://www.woodsmith.com/article/a-look-at-spiral-flute-router-bits/)).
- Harvey Tool's square **down-cut wood end mill** is "engineered to prevent tear-outs and splintering on the top of the workpiece" ([Harvey Tool](https://www.harveytool.com/products/end-mills-for-wood---square-downcut)).
- A furniture maker writing on CNC tooling uses "downcutting mills almost exclusively when cutting hardwood furniture parts", with shallower passes because the waste packs into the cut. Compression bits also work in solid wood "if the cut is going to be over .250″ on the first pass" ([Popular Woodworking: CNC tooling basics, part 4](https://popularwoodworking.com/cnc/cnc-tooling-basics-part-four-mills-and-bits-for-cutting-wood-parts)).
- **Compression bits need a first pass deeper than the up-cut part**, otherwise the top is cut by up-cut flutes and tears like an up-cut bit. "The dado needs to be deeper than the upcut to get a chip free dado". Mortise compression bits have a shorter up-cut ([WOODWEB](https://woodweb.com/knowledge_base/Upcut_Downcut_Or_Compression_Tools.html)). A 0.6–0.9 mm stepdown on an 800 W hobby spindle cannot meet that with a standard compression bit (up-cut length typically several mm; not verified for a specific bit), so on this machine **a down-cut bit is the practical top-edge tool**.
- Amana's solid-wood compression chart: 18 000 rpm, depth of cut 1 × D, **1/4″ 1-flute at .0031″ (0.079 mm)/tooth, 60 IPM (1524 mm/min)**, ramp feed 60 IPM. Feed −25 % at 2 × D and −50 % at 3 × D ([Amana chart PDF](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/1558/5651/files/Solid-Carbide-Compression-Spirals-for-Solid-Wood-v5.pdf)).
- Common practice: rough with one geometry and finish with the other. Rough with an up-cut (good chip clearing), then finish the walls with a down-cut. One reference reports the reverse order for the first pass ([search summary of FineWoodworking and WOODWEB forum threads](https://finewoodworking.com/forum/upcut-or-downcut-that-is-the-question); forum experience, not a maker's data).

### 2.2 Climb vs conventional, grain

- "Climb cutting on the finishing pass usually produces a cleaner edge in wood than conventional cutting". It needs rigid workholding ([Laguna](https://info.lagunatools.com/how-to-prevent-tearout-on-a-wood-cnc-router); same in [Laguna's hardwood guide](https://info.lagunatools.com/cnc-milling-wood-a-guide-to-hardwood-tooling-feeds-and-clean-cuts) and [CNCCookbook tip 3](https://www.cnccookbook.com/16-cnc-router-tips-to-avoid-tearout-and-splintering): conventional cutting along the grain "rips out fibres").
- Production rule of thumb: "conventional cut panel products and climb cut solids". The grain matters: climb with the grain, and take care climbing across end grain on small parts. Test both directions and look at the waste side: "if the waste cut looks better than the good part, reverse the direction". One shop climbs the roughing pass and finishes conventionally to remove the deflection lip ([WOODWEB: climb vs conventional](https://woodweb.com/knowledge_base/Climb_Cutting_Versus_Conventional_Cutting.html)). CNCCookbook recommends a conventional finishing pass when the setup is "deflection challenged" ([CNCCookbook](https://s3.amazonaws.com/s3.cnccookbook.com/CCCNCMillFeedsSpeedsClimbConventional.htm)).
- **Cut end grain before long grain**, so the long-grain pass cleans up the breakout at the corners ([Laguna](https://info.lagunatools.com/how-to-prevent-tearout-on-a-wood-cnc-router), [CNCCookbook tip 9](https://www.cnccookbook.com/16-cnc-router-tips-to-avoid-tearout-and-splintering)).

### 2.3 Chip load: too low rubs, too high splits

- "Feed rate that is too low forces the bit to rub instead of cut", giving heat and **fuzzy edges** ([Laguna](https://info.lagunatools.com/how-to-prevent-tearout-on-a-wood-cnc-router)). Powdery dust and a squeal are signs of rubbing ([search summary, Carbide 3D community and CNCCookbook](https://community.carbide3d.com/t/origin-consistency-of-chipload-recommendations/14152?page=8)).
- Maker charts for a 1/4″ bit in hardwood at 18 000 rpm: .005–.007″ (0.127–0.178 mm) per tooth for Onsrud's 52-200/57-200 (57-200 is Onsrud's down-cut series) and Amana's spiral plunge chart. Reduce 25 % at 2 × D depth and 50 % at 3 × D ([Onsrud Hard Wood chart](https://onsrud.com/images/Hard%20Wood.pdf) as cited in [feeds-speeds.md](../architecture/feeds-speeds.md#material-data-v01); [Amana spiral plunge solid wood](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/1558/5651/files/Solid-Carbide-Spiral-Plunge-Solid-Wood-v5.pdf)). The user's program is at 0.157 mm, inside the range.
- A light finishing pass must still make a real chip, not dust ([search summary](https://community.carbide3d.com/t/origin-consistency-of-chipload-recommendations/14152?page=8); radial chip thinning: [CNCCookbook](https://s3.amazonaws.com/s3.cnccookbook.com/CCChipThinning.htm)).
- On hobby machines the feed cap makes the maker's chip load hard to reach with a 1/4″ bit at router speeds ([Sienci forum](https://forum.sienci.com/t/my-go-to-feed-and-speed-settings-for-hardwood-on-longmill/26370), user report). Fewer flutes is the usual answer: one flute at 18 000 rpm and 3000 mm/min gives 0.167 mm. This is arithmetic from the formulas the makers print, not a maker recommendation.

### 2.4 Passes: shallow first pass, finishing allowance, ramp

- **Rough, then finish with a small allowance**: "0.020 to 0.040 inch is typical" (0.5–1.0 mm) for a slower finishing pass. Several shallow passes, "half the bit diameter or less per pass". A **shallow scoring cut** around the outline "severs the top fibers before the main cut reaches them". Ramp or helix in rather than plunging. Lead-in and lead-out arcs avoid dwell marks ([Laguna](https://info.lagunatools.com/how-to-prevent-tearout-on-a-wood-cnc-router)). Carbide 3D users suggest about a 0.04″ (1 mm) finishing contour, and offsetting the pocket and finishing with a contour to stop splintering ([Carbide 3D community: wood splintering](https://community.carbide3d.com/t/wood-splintering/86120)).
- **Shallower cuts engage less of the helix**, so an up-cut pulls the top fibres up less ([CNCCookbook tip 10](https://www.cnccookbook.com/16-cnc-router-tips-to-avoid-tearout-and-splintering)). That matters for the full-depth finishing pass of § 1 item 4.
- Down-cut and compression bits should reach depth quickly rather than ramp shallowly. Up-cut bits ramp ([CNCCookbook tip 8](https://www.cnccookbook.com/16-cnc-router-tips-to-avoid-tearout-and-splintering)).

### 2.5 Rigidity: stickout, flute length, sharpness

- Deflection goes with the cube of unsupported length. "Doubling the unsupported length of a cutter can reduce stiffness by roughly eight times". "Choose the shortest flute length that comfortably clears the required depth of cut". Chatter gives inconsistent chips and a poor finish ([ToolsToday: flute length, stickout and rigidity](https://global.toolstoday.com/learn/flute-length-stickout-and-rigidity-the-underrated-variables-in-cnc-router-cutting); same rule from Harvey: [In the Loupe](https://www.harveyperformance.com/in-the-loupe/tag/roughing/)).
- "Long, skinny bits deflect under load and produce tearout the larger, shorter equivalent would not." "A sharp bit slices cleanly. A dull bit tears." For hardwood, "prioritize sharp tooling" ([Laguna](https://info.lagunatools.com/how-to-prevent-tearout-on-a-wood-cnc-router)). Fuzz "pretty much everywhere you cut" usually means a new bit is needed ([CNCCookbook](https://www.cnccookbook.com/16-cnc-router-tips-to-avoid-tearout-and-splintering)).

### 2.6 Surface preparation and clean-up

- **Masking tape** on the visible face is "cheap, surprisingly effective" ([Laguna](https://info.lagunatools.com/how-to-prevent-tearout-on-a-wood-cnc-router), [CNCCookbook tip 12](https://www.cnccookbook.com/16-cnc-router-tips-to-avoid-tearout-and-splintering)). **A coat or two of finish** (shellac, varnish, poly) before cutting stiffens the surface fibres ([CNCCookbook tip 11](https://www.cnccookbook.com/16-cnc-router-tips-to-avoid-tearout-and-splintering); Carbide 3D users report dewaxed shellac helps, others see little difference: [Carbide 3D community](https://community.carbide3d.com/t/do-this-and-you-will-be-happy/52262)). Sealing first can affect how a stain takes later (forum reports).
- Leftover fuzz is sanded off, with a sanding mop in tight corners ([Sienci: sanding & finishing](https://resources.sienci.com/view/lm-project-finishing/)).
- **Leave a skin and face last.** If the part's top sits a little below the stock top, a final facing pass removes the torn layer. This is shop practice; no maker source was found for it (not verified as a cited rule). Facing that runs off an edge into an open cavity can itself chip that edge, so a down-cut or climb facing and a light skin (≤ 0.5 mm) suit it best.

## 3. What to do on the next cut

These steps use the current tools and Setup 2's numbers, roughly in order of payoff per minute of effort.

1. **Shorten the extralong bit's stickout from 62 to about 36 mm** (31.04 mm cut + ~5 mm for the collet nut). Set the plan operation's stickout to match. That makes the bit about 5× stiffer, and this matters most at the top levels. If the cavity is ever cut with this bit again (the earlier export did), the same applies there.
2. **Raise the finishing allowance from 0.2 to 0.5 mm** on the Cavity pocket and the Outline profile (strategy `finishAllowance`). Roughing tearout then stays inside the layer the finishing pass removes. Keep `direction` on climb, the default, for the first try. If one wall still tears, test conventional on that wall's grain direction in scrap (§ 2.2).
3. **Split the Cavity's finishing pass into levels:** set `finishStepdown` to about 10 mm instead of the full 30.54 mm. Less of the up-cut helix is engaged at the top edge, and the pass also stays within the 20 mm flutes. Check the flute length in the library: if the bit's flutes really are 20 mm, the bottom ~10 mm of the 30.5 mm cavity wall is rubbed by the shank.
4. **Tape the top face** with wide painter's tape before Setup 2, or brush on one thin coat of dewaxed shellac and let it dry. Zeroing on tape adds its thickness (~0.1 mm) to Z. Take Z0 before taping, or on a bare spot.
5. **Keep the feeds the calculator gave**: cavity 6350 rpm / 3000 mm/min (0.157 mm/tooth), outline 9525 rpm / 3000 mm/min. Do not lower the feed or raise the rpm to "be gentle". At 18 000 rpm and 3000 mm/min the 3-flute bit gets 0.056 mm/tooth, below the hardwood range: it rubs, heats and fuzzes (§ 2.3). If the spindle audibly bogs at 6350 rpm, lower the stepdown, not the rpm.
6. **Optional, better finish wall:** use the **Wexme 1/4″ 1F** for the Cavity at about 17 000–18 000 rpm and 3000 mm/min (0.167–0.176 mm/tooth, inside the range, at the 18 000 rpm the router-bit charts assume, where the spindle has more power). This changes the tool on the cavity operation (Ø6.22 instead of 6.33). The calculator picks these numbers itself in auto mode once the tool changes.
7. **Clean up:** a light sand of the top edges (180–240 grit block, or a sanding mop in corners). Optionally run the 90° V-bit by hand along the edges for a 0.5 mm chamfer. DFYOM can chamfer only circular edges today (§ 4).
8. **Buy for next time:** a 1/4″ **down-cut** spiral (2-flute, solid carbide, wood geometry; for example Onsrud 57-200 series or a Harvey wood down-cut) for the top-edge finishing passes. Down-cut bits pack chips, so use them for the finishing pass (0.5 mm radial) and the first few millimetres, not for slotting 31 mm deep. A compression bit pays off only when the first pass can go deeper than its up-cut part, which is unlikely at 0.6–0.9 mm stepdowns on an 800 W spindle.
9. **If the design allows**, make the part 0.5 mm thinner than the stock on the top side and add a facing operation last in Setup 2, after the cavity and before the outline frees the part. It removes the torn layer entirely (§ 2.6). DFYOM's default order puts facing first within a tool, so move it. Z re-zeroing on a faced top is the open DF69.

## Not verified

- That the user's bits are up-cut (generic end mills are right-hand cut, right-hand spiral; not checked on the physical bits).
- Which export was cut (the newest, 23:01, is assumed; the 19:15 one cut the cavity with the 62 mm extralong bit).
- Typical up-cut length of standard 1/4″ compression bits (no maker's dimension read).
- "Face last with a skin" as a cited rule: shop practice, no maker source found.
- The Onsrud Hard Wood chart was not re-read for this note (its PDF text is not extractable here); its values are as cited in [feeds-speeds.md](../architecture/feeds-speeds.md#material-data-v01).
